Value index — distance to the price curve

We fit a price curve — log₁₀(cost) = g(quality), what a given quality typically costs across all __NCOUPLES__ measured couples — not just the Pareto frontier, but each one weighted by 1 − d/dmax, where d is how many decades dearer it is than the cheapest couple reaching at least its quality. A couple on the frontier counts fully, the single worst one not at all, everything in between on a straight line. Fitting all couples flat would let strictly-dominated models steer the curve; fitting the frontier alone would discard the measurements that populate the middle (R² ≈ …, drawn faint grey above). Each couple's distance in log-cost to that curve says how much cheaper or dearer it is than the frontier price for its quality. Measuring in cost (not quality) keeps the weighting linear where the curve flattens. That distance becomes the value index by exponentiating it relative to the anchor : 100 = __ANCHOR__, and because it is a ratio rather than a stretched scale, 350 reads literally as 3.5× the anchor's value for money and 45 as 0.45×. It is unbounded above — the price of an interpretable multiple. Centres only : the curve and every index use each couple's central value; smearing a point over its interval through these non-linear scales would pull it toward parity, a bias rather than an uncertainty. The curve is monotone by construction — the price of quality cannot fall as quality rises — which also makes it safe to let it extrapolate freely beyond the observed range: it can flatten or steepen, never fold back. Its shape carries a growing curvature term, because cost climbs gently across most of the quality range then steepens sharply near the ceiling — a plain parabola cannot follow that, and an unconstrained one bends back on itself and flips the sign of every index around it. This grading is also what gives the curve its bend : fitted flat over every couple it comes out almost straight, because the dilated axes already linearise most of the cloud ; weighted toward the frontier it follows the sharp rise near the quality ceiling, where efficient couples actually sit. Only the Pareto-frontier couples are shown.

Best value by task tier — what to actually run

Consolidating the frontier into a decision. Four target complexity levels q* are derived from the data, not fixed: they spread evenly across the frontier's own quality span in the dilated metric, so the bottom tier tracks the weakest couple available and the top tier sits exactly on the best one — as models improve, the windows move with them instead of aiming at a level fixed at some past release. The width follows the spacing on one rule : adjacent windows cross at half weight midway between their centres, below the target. Each window is a half bell : below its target a couple is penalised by the Gaussian, at or above it the couple clears the bar and earns a small bonus that saturates fast (at most +20 %), so being better never hurts. For each tier we then pick the Pareto-frontier couple maximising that window times its value index, with cost raised to a per-tier exponent γ — 1.20 · 1.05 · 0.95 · 0.80 from throwaway work up to research-grade. Cost does not weigh the same at every complexity : on grunt work you want the cheapest thing that clears the bar, on a hard task a few extra points of capability are worth paying for. γ > 1 punishes cost more than proportionally, γ < 1 less. Only the selection is tilted : the index shown on a card stays the neutral γ = 1 one, so the four cards remain comparable with each other and with the anchor. Cost enters through the index, i.e. in log-cost like everywhere else in this report : a raw quality ÷ cost ratio would be driven almost entirely by cost, which spans a factor __COSTSPAN__ across the cloud against 2 for quality. Sliders below override both. The big figure is the pick's value index, the same one used throughout the report : 100 = the anchor, __ANCHOR__, and the value reads as a multiple of it — 350 means 3.5× the anchor's value for money. The anchor is read from every couple, dominated ones included, not just the frontier : a new model can push it off the frontier — Opus 5.5, which holds every frontier point but Haiku 4.5's, does — but never out of the full set, so the reference survives a release. The crown is chosen on a different criterion, local prominence along the frontier, and shows the same index.

What moves everything — four factors behind the numbers

The tier picks above are an order of magnitude. Four factors, each measured elsewhere, modulate them strongly — keep them in mind when reading the tiers and the matrix.

Task type

Effort and model choice don't weigh the same depending on the nature of the work — review, reasoning and pure coding reward effort very differently.

Complexity

The harder the task, the more effort costs — up to the point where extra thinking starts to hurt (the inverted-U).

Generation

Each release buys the same quality for less — Sonnet 5.5 beats Sonnet 5's max quality at its own high effort for about a fifth of the cost, Opus 5.5 beats Fable 5.1's max quality at its own high effort for about a sixth of the cost, Fable 5.1 beats Fable 5's max quality at its own xHigh effort for about three quarters of the cost, and Opus 5 reaches Fable-5-class quality for a little over half the cost per task; Sonnet 5 is the verbose exception, cheap per token but talkative.

Cache & harness

Two invisible factors — cache-read rate and the surrounding agent harness — that can dominate both cost and score.

How the numbers are built — the method

Every value in this report is computed from the raw measurements by one procedure — nothing is hand-entered. The full methodology is in METHODOLOGY.md (from collection to the fit) and DISPLAY-METHODOLOGY.md (from the fit to the tier picks); the validation scripts are in model/validation.

In short : scores from different benchmarks do not share a scale — a benchmark near its ceiling compresses differences, a hard one inflates them — so the report never averages raw scores or score ratios. A latent-quality model estimates, for every benchmark, its own zero and its own gain, and for every (model, effort) couple one quality and one cost. Each couple stays atomic: no model×effort separability is assumed.

The procedure

  1. Same task, same configuration. Measurements are compared only within a group — one source, one task, one harness, one setting, one scale; a group that mixes them is split. The effort level is read from the source's configuration, never assumed; runs without a known effort stay out of the fit.
  2. Each metric on its natural scale. A bounded score (a percentage, a score out of N) goes through a logit, which removes floor and ceiling compression; money and points go through a log; an Elo or an unknown composite is left as is; cost goes through a log. The rule reads the metric's label, never the benchmark's identity. On the logit scale a score near 0 % or 100 % is noisier, and weighs accordingly. Every value carries its reading precision: the rounding of a printed number, and for a value read off a chart, its resolution and the chart's own error (measured on runs drawn on two charts, about 2 % on a digitised cost).
  3. One model for all groups. Every group gets its own offset, gain and noise, so the unit and zero of a metric never matter. A couple's latent value is shared by all groups; on top of it, a publisher's systematic effect on a couple and on a model (shared by the model's effort levels) and a couple's deviation on a task type are estimated, so a publisher with many benchmarks, or a couple tested mostly on one kind of task, cannot tilt the result. Robust (Student-t) noise whose tails are estimated; early-access runs are down-weighted by an estimated factor. No couple serves as a reference in the fit: the latent values sum to zero. Fitted in Stan, published only if the standard convergence checks pass.
  4. Weights follow from the data. A group of k couples informs the spacing of couples in proportion to its discrimination² × (k − 2); a saturated or noisy benchmark weighs little; a publisher's weight saturates at its own systematic effect. No weight is set per source.
  5. Nothing counts twice. One execution printed twice — a system card reprinting an earlier card's series, the same run drawn on two charts, one run scored with two metrics — is detected and counted once: its score and its cost must both agree, within their reading precisions and a rounding made the wrong way. An index is left out for a couple whose index components are already in the data.
  6. Relative values and intervals. Quality = a couple's expected score averaged over the benchmark panel, divided by __ANCHOR__'s; cost = its cost on a task of typical size, divided by __ANCHOR__'s. The reference couple is a display choice: changing it divides every value by a constant. Each couple carries its own 16–84 % interval, so two couples compare through their two intervals, whatever the reference. A couple measured by a single publisher is not shown.
  7. From the grids to the picks. The Pareto frontier keeps the couples nothing beats on both cost and quality. A monotone price curve — what a given quality typically costs, fitted on every couple with weight 1 − d/dmax by distance d to the frontier — gives each couple a value index, 100 = __ANCHOR__. The four tiers spread their target qualities evenly across the frontier, score frontier couples with a half-bell window around each target times the value index (cost weighted more on routine work, less on research-grade work), and the crown goes to the couple that stands out most from its frontier neighbours.

Key values

  • Set by hand, never tuned to the data: weakly informative priors (half-Student-t(3, 0, 2.5) on every spread, Gamma(2, 0.1) on the noise tails); a logit kept half a step inside 0 and 100 %; a printed value's precision at its rounding (last digit / √12); an effect seen in a single measurement left out; a couple shown only when two publishers measured it; a reprint recognised by values that agree within their precisions, on two values at least and on all they share but one.
  • Uncertainty. Every interval is 16–84 % (one standard deviation): the couple's own uncertainty, whatever the reference.
  • Published only when the fit is sound: R̂ ≤ 1.01 and at least 400 effective draws on every parameter, no divergent transition. The Monte Carlo error of each value is checked against its last displayed digit; in this fit every figure is stable to that digit but one, the cost of Fable 5.1 at max, which could move by one unit (3.09 ± 0.006).
  • Display choices: the quality axis is dilated near parity; the price curve, the value index and the four tiers use constants listed with their reasons in DISPLAY-METHODOLOGY.md.

Techniques and software

  • Bayesian hierarchical model sampled by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the No-U-Turn sampler (Hoffman & Gelman 2014), written in Stan and run with CmdStan (cost) and nutpie (quality); built and checked stage by stage following the Bayesian workflow.
  • Convergence checked with the rank-normalised R̂ and effective sample sizes of Vehtari et al. 2021, computed with ArviZ.
  • Intervals as quasi-standard errors (Firth & de Menezes 2004), so that any two couples compare through their own intervals.
  • Scales: the empirical logit of bounded scores, with the binomial shape of its noise.

Checks. Scores held out of the fit and predicted: median error 1.96 points, against 4.78 for averaging score ratios; the 16–84 % predictive interval covers 66 % of them. On synthetic data with known qualities, pairs of couples in the wrong order: 0.1–0.8 % (0.4–2.2 % for score ratios). Without the largest publisher, the model vendor itself, costs move by 2.4 % and qualities by 0.9 % at the median. Details and scripts: METHODOLOGY.md.
