Value — distance to the price trend

The price trend — ln(cost) = a + λ·θ, θ the latent quality — is what a given quality costs today across the 4 couples of the latest model of each family, not just the frontier: it is the going rate of the models on sale. Earlier generations, dearer per point than their successors, would flatten it and make quality look cheap; it does not move when older models are shown (R² ≈ 0.88, drawn faint grey above). Each couple weighs by its uncertainty across the line, both axes counted. On the latent scale the trend is a straight line: each step of quality costs the same ratio more, wherever it sits. A couple's distance to it says how many times cheaper or dearer it is than the trend charges for its quality — the same gap read on the other axis is how much more quality it gives than the trend at its cost. No couple serves as a reference. Only the frontier couples are listed.

Best value by task tier — what to actually run

Consolidating the frontier into a decision. Four target qualities are derived from the data, not fixed: they are the 20th, 40th, 60th and 80th percentiles of the quality of the couples shown on the page, so that the four tiers sit across the bulk of today's couples — as models improve, the targets move with them, and turning makers or models off takes them again on what is shown. They depend on quality only, not on prices. The width follows the mean spacing of the targets on one rule : adjacent windows cross at half weight midway between their targets. For each tier we pick, among the frontier couples, the one that maximises window × eβ·λθ ⁄ cost, λ the slope of the price trend and β the tier's price of quality as a multiple of the market's : grunt work pays a quarter of it (β = 0.25), everyday tasks 0.63, advanced reasoning 1.59, cutting-edge thinking four times (β = 4) — evenly spread in ratio, symmetric about the market. Below its target a couple is penalised by a Gaussian window ; at or above it the window is 1, and eβ·λθ ⁄ cost rewards smoothly a couple that brings more quality than the tier is ready to pay for its extra cost. Cost counts in ratios, as people perceive prices : twice as dear weighs the same at every price. The big figure is the pick's value index : 100 = the price trend, 500 means five times cheaper than the trend charges for its quality, 50 twice as dear. The crown is the frontier couple that sits furthest below the trend. Sliders below override the targets, widths and prices of quality.

What moves everything — four factors behind the numbers

The tier picks above are an order of magnitude. Four factors, each measured elsewhere, modulate them strongly — keep them in mind when reading the tiers and the matrix.

Task type

Effort and model choice don't weigh the same depending on the nature of the work — review, reasoning and pure coding reward effort very differently.

Complexity

The harder the task, the more effort costs — up to the point where extra thinking starts to hurt (the inverted-U).

Generation

Each release buys the same quality for less: turn on Older models to set each family's earlier models beside its current one, on the same scales.

Cache & harness

Two invisible factors — cache-read rate and the surrounding agent harness — that can dominate both cost and score.

How the numbers are built — the method

Every value in this report is computed from the raw measurements by one procedure — nothing is hand-entered. The full methodology is in METHODOLOGY.md (from collection to the fit) and DISPLAY-METHODOLOGY.md (from the fit to the tier picks); the validation scripts are in model/validation.

In short : scores from different benchmarks do not share a scale — a benchmark near its ceiling compresses differences, a hard one inflates them — so the report never averages raw scores or score ratios. A latent-quality model estimates, for every benchmark, its own zero and its own gain, and for every (model, effort) couple one quality and one cost. Each couple stays atomic: no model×effort separability is assumed.

The procedure

  1. Every number traced to its source. Each source is recorded as facts: the value as the source writes it, the exact text or table cell it is read from, the URL and the date of the capture; agents read every value line by line, and a measurement whose facts lack their proof stays out. Registries of models (the names each was served under, its effort settings and defaults, dated), routes (makers' APIs, resellers, routers, harnesses), benchmarks and their versions, publishers and dated prices let one build decide, by a fixed decision tree, which model, configuration and price each measurement belongs to.
  2. Same task, same configuration. Measurements are compared only within a group — one source, one task, one harness, one setting, one scale, one version of a board; a group that mixes them is split. Every run is dated, and its model and effort are read as the maker and every route in between served them on that date — never assumed: a route the source does not name is never presumed to be the maker's API, and every route that could have served the run must give the same configuration. At the least doubt on the model, the effort or what was run, the measurement stays in the data file and out of the fit.
  3. Costs at today's prices. Every cost is put on the model's current price from the prices in force on its run date: the source's own figure at that price, the price change applied to the run's own token counts, or, when the token mix is unknown, a price band whose width is added to the cost's precision. A cost that cannot be brought to the current price stays out.
  4. Each metric on its natural scale. A bounded score (a percentage, a score out of N) goes through a logit, which removes floor and ceiling compression; money and points go through a log; an Elo or an unknown composite is left as is; cost goes through a log. The scale is read from the metric's label, never from the benchmark's identity; whether lower is better is read from the benchmark's registry, and such a score is turned in its own unit first (an attack success rate of 30 % reads 70 %); a score whose direction is not established stays out. On the logit scale a score near 0 % or 100 % is noisier, and weighs accordingly. Every value carries its reading precision: the rounding of a printed number, and for a value read off a chart, its resolution and the chart's own error (measured on runs drawn on two charts, about 2 % on a digitised cost).
  5. One model for all groups. Every group gets its own offset, gain and noise, so the unit and zero of a metric never matter. A couple's latent value is shared by all groups; on top of it, a publisher's systematic effect on a couple and on a model (shared by the model's effort levels) and a couple's deviation on a task type are estimated, so a publisher with many benchmarks, or a couple tested mostly on one kind of task, cannot tilt the result. The sizes of these effects are estimated for each model maker on its own, not tied to the other makers'; a maker's own charts about its own models carry an estimated home effect, also each maker's own, left out of every value. The fit takes the couples of eight makers measured in the same groups (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, xAI, DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM, Kimi), on one scale; this page shows the DeepSeek couples. Robust (Student-t) noise whose tails are estimated; early-access runs are down-weighted by an estimated factor. No couple serves as a reference in the fit: the latent values sum to zero. Fitted in Stan, published only if the standard convergence checks pass.
  6. Weights follow from the data. A group of k couples informs the spacing of couples in proportion to its discrimination² × (k − 2); a saturated or noisy benchmark weighs little; a publisher's weight saturates at its own systematic effect. No weight is set per source; the one weight set by rule is that of a single run: when a source gives k quality measurements of one run (several metrics, sub-scores or slices of the same executions), each weighs 1/k, so the run counts as one measurement.
  7. Nothing counts twice. One execution printed twice — a system card reprinting an earlier card's series, the same run drawn on two charts, a board copying another's numbers, one run scored with two metrics — is detected and counted once: its score and its cost must both agree, within their reading precisions and a rounding made the wrong way. Versions of one board that re-grade the same runs (recognised by identical costs) count those runs once. Before the fit, two groups that still share identical costs on three couples or more stop it until the copy is resolved or the coincidence proven from the sources. An index keeps, for a couple whose index components are already in the data, only what its missing components add, or is left out.
  8. Values and intervals. Quality = a couple's latent quality θ, shown as the score it predicts averaged over the benchmark panel; cost = its cost on a task of typical size, shown as a multiple of the cheapest couple. No couple serves as a reference, in the fit or on the page. Each couple carries its own 16–84 % interval, so two couples compare through their two intervals. A couple measured by a single publisher is not shown.
  9. From the fit to the picks. Every decision is taken on the latent scale (log cost, θ). The Pareto frontier keeps the couples nothing beats on both axes; they are the candidates of every pick. A price trend — what a given quality costs today, fitted on the latest model of each family — says how many times cheaper or dearer each couple is than the going rate. The four tiers set their targets at the 20th, 40th, 60th and 80th percentiles of the quality of the couples shown and pick, among the frontier couples, around each target, the couple that brings the most quality for its cost, each tier paying for quality from a quarter of the market price (grunt work) to four times it (cutting-edge thinking); the crown goes to the couple furthest below the trend.

Key values

  • Set by hand, never tuned to the data: weakly informative priors (half-Student-t(3, 0, 2.5) on every spread, Gamma(2, 0.1) on the noise tails); a logit kept half a step inside 0 and 100 %; a printed value's precision at its rounding (last digit / √12); an effect seen in a single measurement left out; a couple shown only when two publishers measured it; a reprint recognised by values that agree within their precisions, on two values at least and on all they share but one.
  • Uncertainty. Every interval is 16–84 % (one standard deviation): the couple's own uncertainty.
  • Published only when the fit is sound: R̂ ≤ 1.01 and at least 400 effective draws on every parameter, no divergent transition. The Monte Carlo error of each value is checked against its last displayed digit; in this fit one figure on this page can move by one unit in that digit (the cost of DeepSeek V4 Pro preview at high), far inside their intervals.
  • Display choices: the quality axis is compressed below the weakest tier target; the frontier, the price trend and the four tiers use constants listed with their reasons in DISPLAY-METHODOLOGY.md.

Techniques and software

  • Bayesian hierarchical model sampled by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the No-U-Turn sampler (Hoffman & Gelman 2014), written in Stan and run with nutpie on both axes, six chains whose draws continue in the same run until the criteria are met (CmdStan only to resume a stopped fit from its stored draws, or to restart a chain left in a far region from a good chain's last draw); built and checked stage by stage following the Bayesian workflow.
  • Convergence checked with the rank-normalised R̂ and effective sample sizes of Vehtari et al. 2021, computed with ArviZ.
  • Intervals as quasi-standard errors (Firth & de Menezes 2004), so that any two couples compare through their own intervals.
  • Scales: the empirical logit of bounded scores, with the binomial shape of its noise.
